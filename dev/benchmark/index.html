    Benchmark¶

    This page reports how Mohar's trust rules behave on simulated agents, next to three simpler ways of deciding the same actions. The harness is crates/mohar-bench in the repository; the tables under Results are written by it, and a test fails when they no longer match what it produces. Design section 10 lists what the benchmark is meant to measure.

    Method¶

    Twenty simulated agents work for 63 simulated days on three capabilities, six actions per capability per day, which is 181,440 actions per policy over eight seeds. The capabilities are edit (low risk, reversible), deps (medium risk, reversible) and deploy (high risk, irreversible, never above Supervised).

    The agents come in four kinds, five of each, with a fixed real pass rate: good (98%), middling (87%), poor (75%) and drifting (98% for four weeks, then 70%). Whether an action passes is decided by hashing the seed, the agent, the capability and the action number, so every policy sees the same tasks.

    When a policy asks a person, the person stops half of the failing actions before they run and refuses them as wrong, which counts against the agent as a minor failure, as mohar refuse --wrong does in the daemon. Everything that runs is verified. A failure that ran is an incident.

    A promotion is false when the agent's real pass rate at that moment is below what the level stands for: 80% for Supervised and 95% for Trusted. Time to demotion counts the actions a drifting agent ran at its old level after its quality dropped, until it was demoted.

    The prediction part is separate. One simulated person is asked about 400 edit escalations per seed, with a fixed yes rate per file (100%, 99%, 97%, 95% and 20%). The answer model learns only from answers the person gave, and one prediction in twenty still goes to the person as a check. The simulation knows what the person would have said, so agreement is measured on every prediction, not only on the checks.

    Baselines¶

    • always_ask: every action goes to a person.
    • static_allowlist: edit runs without asking; everything else goes to a person.
    • aggregate_score: Mohar's promotion and demotion rules over one score per agent, pooled across its capabilities, the way a single trust score works.
    • mohar: the same rules with one score per agent per capability (mohar_core::trust::Ledger).

    Prediction agreement: the answer model answered 326 of 3200 escalations (10.2%). 7 of its predictions still went to the person as checks, and 100.0% of the checks agreed. Over every prediction there were 18.4 wrong yes answers per 1,000. Taken seed by seed, the mean was 5.9, with a 95% interval of 0.0 to 19.9.

    Reading the numbers¶

    Against always asking, Mohar removed about 12% of prompts, and 1.7 more failures per 1,000 actions ran. The static allowlist removed a third of the prompts, and 23 more failures per 1,000 ran than under always asking, because it lets every edit through whatever the agent's record.

    The single aggregate score removed far fewer prompts than per-capability trust did (about 4%), most likely because one score mixes an agent's good work on one capability with its failures on another. Its false promotion rate was a little lower than Mohar's in this run. Its median time to demotion was shorter, but it measured only 18 drops. In 102 of the 120 drops in quality the drifting agent had never been promoted, so there was nothing to demote.

    Irreversible failures are the same under every policy. Mohar sends an irreversible high-risk action to a person whatever the agent's level, and the three baselines ask about deploy every time too.

    Before #228 the answer model gave 26 wrong yes answers per 1,000 predictions, more than its 0.98 threshold suggests, and every check agreed. The cause was the calibration. Platt scaling is monotone in the model's score, and this person's answers fall into two groups: CI at 20% yes, everything else between 95% and 100%. The fit placed the line between the groups well but could not tell the paths inside the mostly-yes group apart, so src/cli.rs (95%) was scored like docs/ (100%). The model's probability is now capped at the person's own smoothed yes rate on the capability in the target's top directory, (yes + 1) / (answers + 2). Wrong yes answers fell to the figure above, and the model answers about half as many escalations as before. The remaining wrong answers are on src/cli.rs, which shares src/ with a 97% path, so the cap sees one directory at about 96%. Checks still can't show this: with one prediction in twenty checked, a few hundred predictions give a handful of checks.

    Limits¶

    • The agents are simulated. Their pass rates are fixed and their outcomes independent; real agents have streaks, and their failures cluster on certain tasks.
    • The person stops the same share of failures on every action, and their yes answers are independent draws at fixed rates. Real people get tired, change their minds and miss more on some actions than on others.
    • Eight seeds give wide intervals on the rare events, false promotions above all. The intervals are over per-seed means; they don't pair policies on the same tasks, so the differences between policies are better estimated than the overlapping intervals suggest.
    • Poisoning that reaches durable memory, listed in the roadmap for this phase, isn't measured yet.
    • There are no results from real agents or real reviewers yet.

    Regenerating¶

    MOHAR_WRITE_BENCH=1 cargo test --release -p mohar-bench

    This rewrites crates/mohar-bench/results.json and the tables above. Run it twice: the first run fails because it compares against the files as they were before it wrote them. A change to the trust rules that moves a number has to commit the new numbers with it.
